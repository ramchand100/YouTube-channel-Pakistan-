## Central question

What does Pakistan actually lose, in real economic terms, when a
publicly-trained doctor, engineer, or IT professional leaves for good?

## Tentative thesis

Skilled emigration is a direct, quantifiable loss for Pakistan: the state
spends real money training these professionals, then loses that investment
entirely when they leave, along with their tax contribution and workforce
capacity. The usual "but remittances make up for it" defense doesn't hold
for this specific group — Pakistan's actual remittance lifeline runs
overwhelmingly through Gulf labour migration, not the skilled professionals
this story is about.

## Main entities and institutions

Bureau of Emigration & Overseas Employment (BE&OE), State Bank of Pakistan
(remittance data by source country), Pakistan Medical Commission (PMC),
Pakistan Engineering Council (PEC), public medical and engineering
universities, Ministry of Health (workforce-shortage data), Overseas
Employment Corporation.

## Evidence required

- BE&OE registered skilled/professional-category emigrant numbers
  (multi-year trend, by destination).
- Public medical/engineering college per-seat subsidized training cost
  (HEC or provincial health/education department budget documents, not
  just public estimates).
- Healthcare/engineering workforce-shortage data — doctor-to-population
  ratio, rural vacancy rates, PEC-registered engineer emigration vs.
  domestic retention.
- SBP remittances by source country (multi-year), to establish Gulf
  labour's actual share versus skilled-emigrant-destination countries
  (US/UK/Canada/Australia).
- Any available data or estimate on tax revenue foregone from departed
  high-earning professionals.

## Pakistani economic relevance

Connects to two concrete mechanisms: the fiscal cost of publicly subsidized
professional training being exported for free when the graduate leaves, and
the healthcare/engineering capacity shortfall this creates domestically
(rural clinics, understaffed engineering roles). It also directly tests a
common public assumption — that remittances offset the loss — against
Pakistan's actual remittance-source data.

## Audience consequence

A viewer should walk away with a specific, numbers-backed understanding of
what skilled emigration costs the state (not just an emotional "brain
drain is sad" framing), and should have the common "but they send money
back" defense either confirmed or corrected with real data rather than
assumption.

## Deliberate exclusions

Gulf labour migrants' own migration story as its own narrative thread (used
here only as supporting evidence for the remittance-source point, not a
parallel story); any policy recommendation to restrict emigration; diaspora
investment schemes (already logged separately as the Roshan Digital Account
topic idea).

## Main weakness / research risk

Official data does not break remittances down by the sender's occupation or
emigration date, so the claim that skilled emigrants' remittances don't
meaningfully offset their training-investment loss will need to be built
from proxies (source-country remittance shares, BE&OE occupation-category
registration numbers) rather than a single direct statistic. The research
stage must be precise about which specific claim the evidence supports:
that skilled emigrants remit less per person, or simply that Gulf labour
migrants vastly outnumber them — these are different findings and the
thesis must not overstate whichever one turns out to be true. Per-seat
subsidized training costs may also require budget documents that aren't
publicly summarized anywhere; if they can't be sourced to a defensible
figure, this becomes an [ESTIMATE] with method shown, not a bare number.

## Story logic

Financial / Institutional — the fiscal mechanics of subsidized training
combined with the workforce-capacity consequence.

## Proposed structure (5 parts)

1. **The Anomaly** — open on the concrete cost: what one subsidized
   medical or engineering seat costs the state, set against how many of
   those graduates leave the country.
2. **The Exit** — the scale and mechanism: numbers, destinations, why
   they're leaving (push vs. pull factors), drawn from BE&OE/PMC/PEC data.
3. **What's Left Behind** — the workforce consequence: doctor-to-population
   ratios, rural vacancy rates, what the resulting shortage actually costs
   in service delivery.
4. **The Remittance Defense, Tested** — lay out the "but they send money
   back" argument, then test it directly against SBP source-country data
   showing where remittances actually come from.
5. **The Verdict** — the full picture: training investment lost, workforce
   capacity lost, and a remittance offset that doesn't materialize the way
   it's popularly assumed to.

## Main counterargument

Even if aggregate remittances from skilled emigrants are smaller than from
Gulf labour migrants, their per-capita remittances, diaspora investment, or
eventual return with foreign experience and capital could still represent
real value not captured by a simple aggregate comparison. The script must
name this honestly rather than dismiss it, even though it isn't the thesis
being centered here.
