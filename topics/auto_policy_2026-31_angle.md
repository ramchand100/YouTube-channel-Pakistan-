## Central question

Tariffs are set to fall toward a 6% weighted average by FY2030 and
local-parts protection is capped at roughly five years — does the policy's
own timeline undercut the localization goal it claims to serve?

## Tentative thesis

A genuine attempt to break a decades-old protected, expensive auto sector,
but the built-in tariff phase-down may expose local manufacturers before
they've had time to actually localize — and Pakistan has run this exact
experiment before, under the 2016-21 and 2021-26 auto policies.

## Main entities and institutions

Ministry of Industries and Production, the Engineering Development Board
(EDB), PAAPAM (Pakistan Association of Automotive Parts & Accessories
Manufacturers), existing OEMs (Indus Motors/Toyota, Pak Suzuki, Honda Atlas,
Kia-Lucky Motors, Hyundai Nishat), new EV entrants, used-car importers,
consumers.

## Evidence required

- The actual policy document/final gazetted text — exact year-by-year
  tariff schedule and localization targets, and precisely how the "up to
  five years" protection window is defined per component category.
- How the 2016-21 and 2021-26 auto policies handled the same
  protection-vs-localization tradeoff, and what happened to car prices and
  local-content percentage under them.
- Current Pakistani car prices vs. regional benchmarks, and current
  local-content percentage of major models.
- PAAPAM/OEM public statements on the new policy (support vs. opposition),
  which reveals the distributional stakes.
- EV incentive package specifics, and which models (if any) actually hit
  the reported sub-Rs 1 million price point.

## Pakistani economic relevance

Car affordability is a major middle-class economic issue (financing tied to
the SBP policy rate, competition from used-Japanese imports). The auto
sector is one of Pakistan's largest manufacturing employers and a
long-running symbol of the "protected industry that never grew up" debate.
It also connects to the import bill and FX reserves, since auto parts and
finished vehicles are a persistent import-substitution priority.

## Audience consequence

Consumers should understand whether car prices will actually fall as
promised. Investors/founders in auto-parts manufacturing should understand
the real protection runway before tariffs bite. Policy-watchers should be
able to judge whether this differs from two prior liberalization attempts
that didn't durably fix the sector.

## Deliberate exclusions

EV charging infrastructure as its own topic; the used-car import/grey-market
trade in full depth (context only); consumer auto-financing dynamics as a
separate strand.

## Main weakness / research risk

The policy was reported as a July 2026 draft. Research must verify whether
it was actually finalized/gazetted on schedule or modified before formal
notification — Pakistani policy drafts frequently shift between
announcement and final text, and citing draft-stage provisions that later
changed would undermine the whole episode.

## Story logic

Regulatory / incentive conflict, with a historical comparison to the
2016-21 and 2021-26 auto policies.

## Proposed structure (5 parts)

1. **The Anomaly** — open on the July 2026 policy unveiling and the stark
   fact that decades of "infant industry" protection haven't produced
   globally competitive car prices.
2. **The Mechanism** — how this specific policy works: the tariff schedule,
   the five-year local-parts protection window, used-car import
   liberalization, EV incentives.
3. **The History** — what happened to prices and local content under the
   two previous auto policies; has this exact promise been made and broken
   before?
4. **The Stakes** — winners and losers: OEMs, parts manufacturers,
   consumers, used-car importers.
5. **The Verdict** — whether the timeline realistically lets local
   manufacturers become competitive before protection ends, and what would
   actually need to be true for this policy to break the cycle.

## Main counterargument

Lower tariffs and reduced protection could be read as inherently
pro-consumer regardless of whether local assemblers survive — the
"localization failing" framing assumes protecting them is the right goal,
when faster exposure to import competition might be what actually forces
real localization instead of permanent protection. The script must address
this "protection doesn't work, exposure does" counter-thesis directly, not
dismiss it.
