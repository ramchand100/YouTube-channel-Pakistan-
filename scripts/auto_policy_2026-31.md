## Part 1 — The Anomaly

In July, a Toyota Corolla Cross hybrid got Rs 1.36 million more expensive. Same engine, same badge, same options — just a bigger price tag [SOURCE: Bloom Pakistan, 2026]. Honda's HR-V hybrid went up by a similar amount [SOURCE: Bloom Pakistan, 2026]. Nobody voted for that. No new tax was debated in parliament. So why did it happen — and why, eight weeks later, does the government still not have an answer?

Here's what actually happened. Pakistan's auto policy expired on June 30, and nothing was ready to take its place [SOURCE: ProPakistani, 2026]. Hybrid cars had been taxed at a discounted rate for years. The moment the old policy lapsed, that discount lapsed with it. The tax on every new hybrid snapped back to the same 25 percent everyone else pays [SOURCE: Dawn Business, 2026]. Some dealers even stopped taking hybrid orders for a few days, waiting to see if the government would reverse course [SOURCE: Profit by Pakistan Today, 2026].

It didn't. Weeks later, it still hasn't. A replacement policy exists — in draft — but by early August, the government had quietly shelved its own draft after manufacturers warned it leaned so hard toward electric vehicles that Pakistan risked becoming little more than an assembly floor for imported EV kits [SOURCE: Pakbiz, 2026] [SOURCE: TechJuice, 2026]. A new committee was formed. As of the most recent reporting, that committee has only gone as far as an "in-principle" nod — the policy still hasn't reached the federal cabinet [SOURCE: ProPakistani, 2026] [VERIFY: confirm current status before recording, situation was moving fast as of mid-August].

A government can't finish writing one document, and in the meantime, buyers are the ones paying for the gap. So what does a fight over car tariffs actually have to do with the price tag you're staring at in a showroom right now?

## Part 2 — The Mechanism

Start with what the missing policy is actually supposed to do. Every five years, the government sets two numbers for the car industry: how much tax an imported car or part pays, and how much of a Pakistani-made car actually has to be made in Pakistan.

The draft policy under discussion wants to bring the average tariff down substantially — from roughly 11 rupees of duty for every 100 rupees of value today, to about 7 by 2030, with an even lower target after that [SOURCE: Profit by Pakistan Today, 2026]. A fully imported, ready-to-drive car would be taxed at no more than 15 percent, full stop [SOURCE: PakWheels Blog, 2026] [VERIFY: draft-stage, not finalized]. In exchange, the draft asks for real localization for the first time with actual numbers attached: by 2030, 85 out of every 100 rupees' worth of a small vehicle or motorbike should be made inside the country; for small cars, that climbs to 80 or 90 rupees out of every hundred by 2031 [SOURCE: PakWheels Blog, 2026] [VERIFY: draft-stage]. Parts built specifically for electric vehicles get a head start — just 1 percent customs duty for the first three years, before rising to 5 percent in year four [SOURCE: PakWheels Blog, 2026] [VERIFY: draft-stage]. That's a shorter runway than it sounds: barely four years of real protection before the new-energy-vehicle parts business is exposed to the same competitive pressure everything else faces.

That's the trade the government is trying to make: lower prices sooner, in return for a promise that local manufacturing will actually grow into the gap left behind. It's a reasonable trade on paper. It fell apart in practice because nobody inside the government could agree on how fast to move. The Ministry of Industries wanted to soften the tariff cuts by pairing them with a new excise duty, so the real level of protection barely changed even as the headline tariff number dropped [SOURCE: PakWheels Blog, 2026] [VERIFY]. The Ministry of Commerce held firm: the cut had to happen on schedule, tied to Pakistan's broader commitment to lower tariffs across the whole economy, not just cars [SOURCE: PakWheels Blog, 2026] [VERIFY]. The IMF, watching from outside, rejected one attempted workaround — a special 1 percent sales tax carved out just for electric vehicles — and insisted on the standard 18 percent rate everyone else pays [SOURCE: PakWheels Blog, 2026] [VERIFY: reported secondhand, primary IMF document not directly accessed].

Three parties, three positions, and a policy that expired while they argued. This isn't the first time Pakistan's government has tried to walk this exact line — protect local manufacturers just long enough that they can actually compete, without protecting them so long they never have to. It's worth asking what happened the last two times it tried.

## Part 3 — The History

In 2016, the government didn't even set a percentage target the way this draft does. Instead, it just made importing more expensive than building locally: a Category-A manufacturer paid 10 percent customs duty on parts sourced from abroad, and 25 percent on parts made in Pakistan — backwards from what you'd expect, deliberately, to make local sourcing the cheaper option for five straight years [SOURCE: Board of Investment, Pakistan, primary policy document]. On paper, it worked well enough to attract interest: more than a dozen new manufacturers announced plans to set up in Pakistan [SOURCE: industry analysis, 2026] [VERIFY: figure from industry commentary, not an official tally]. In practice, most of those plans were quietly shelved or never broke ground, as the rupee kept losing value and the rules kept shifting under everyone's feet [SOURCE: industry analysis, 2026] [VERIFY].

The next policy, running from 2021 to 2026, tried a different lever entirely: export targets instead of import duties. The results split right down the middle. Motorcycles and tractors blew past what the policy expected — exporting around one and a half times their target [SOURCE: AutoWorld Journal, 2026] [VERIFY: primary EDB/PAMA export data not directly confirmed]. Cars did not. Exported cars stayed under 1 percent of everything Pakistan actually built in that period — nowhere close to what the policy had promised [SOURCE: AutoWorld Journal, 2026] [VERIFY].

So this fight over tariffs and timelines isn't a new argument dressed up in new numbers. It's the third attempt in a decade to answer the same question with a different tool each time, and neither previous attempt produced a car industry that could stand on its own without protection. That history is exactly why the current stalemate matters more than it looks — and it's exactly why the people fighting over this draft have very different ideas about who should carry the risk this time.

## Part 4 — The Stakes

The parts manufacturers' association, PAAPAM, says the math the government is proposing simply doesn't work. According to PAAPAM, their members carry a 34 percent cost disadvantage against manufacturers in other countries, because Pakistan doesn't produce a single one of the raw materials a car actually needs — no domestic auto-grade steel, no plastic resin, no rubber built for the purpose [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2026]. Every bit of it comes in from somewhere else, stacked on top of expensive electricity, high taxes, and expensive borrowing. That's their case for asking the government to hold import duties at 50 to 60 percent and guarantee at least 45 percent protection on anything made locally — roughly three times higher than what the current draft offers [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2026].

PAAPAM puts real numbers behind the warning, too: more than a million livelihoods, by their count — over 300,000 people employed directly in manufacturing, and another 700,000 depending on the industry indirectly [SOURCE: Profit by Pakistan Today, 2026] [VERIFY: PAAPAM's own estimate, not an independent labour count]. Cut tariffs on the government's proposed timeline, they argue, and the industry doesn't adjust — it collapses.

That argument deserves a real hearing. It also deserves a real counterargument, because it assumes the goal is protecting the manufacturers already in business. There's a defensible case that protection is exactly what's kept Pakistan's parts industry from becoming competitive in the first place. Two policies and roughly a decade of tariff shelter didn't produce the local supply base either one was designed to build. At some point, more time on the same terms starts to look less like a runway and more like a reason to never take off. Neither side has clean evidence to settle which of these is true — that's what makes it a real disagreement, not a solved problem the government is simply refusing to accept. Which side wins that argument is still being decided right now, in a committee room no car buyer will ever see.

## Part 5 — The Verdict

As of the most recent reporting, a revised draft has cleared one internal hurdle — an "in-principle" nod from the Dar-led committee — and is still working its way toward the Ministry of Industries and then the federal cabinet [SOURCE: ProPakistani, 2026] [VERIFY: confirm current status before recording]. Nobody outside that process knows yet whether the final version will lean toward PAAPAM's slower timeline or the tariff schedule Commerce and the IMF are pushing for.

What's different this time is that the cost of waiting isn't hypothetical anymore. It already showed up on a price tag in July, months before anyone in Islamabad had to make a single permanent decision. If the finished policy — whenever it actually clears the cabinet — repeats the same protect-first approach as 2016 and 2021, the honest expectation is the same outcome: a temporarily sheltered industry, prices that don't fall the way the government promises, and another five-year clock that starts this exact argument over again in 2031. For anyone buying a car in Pakistan in the meantime, the price you're looking at right now isn't the price the policy is actually trying to fix — it's the price of the policy not existing yet.
