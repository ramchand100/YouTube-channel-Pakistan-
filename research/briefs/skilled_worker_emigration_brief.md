# Research Brief: The Cost of Skilled-Worker Emigration

- **Angle file:** `topics/skilled_worker_emigration_angle.md`
- **Central question:** What does Pakistan actually lose, in real economic
  terms, when a publicly-trained doctor, engineer, or IT professional
  leaves for good?
- **Research date:** 2026-08-29
- **Data cutoff date:** 2026-08-29

## Central paradox / hook

Pakistan received a record **$41.6 billion** in workers' remittances in
FY2025-26 [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2026] [SOURCE: Gulf News, 2026] —
the kind of figure usually cited as proof that emigration pays for itself.
At almost the same time, a Pakistan Institute of Development Economics
(PIDE)-linked model put the annual opportunity cost of skilled emigration
at **$4.2 billion**, after netting out remittances, with the loss of
gross income to destination economies estimated at **$4.8 billion**
[SOURCE: PIDE, via Profit/Tribune/Daily Times, Jul 2025] [VERIFY: could
not directly open the PIDE report or any of the three outlets reporting
it — beoe.gov.pk and all three news sites returned 403 on direct fetch;
figures are as relayed by WebSearch summaries of those articles, not
independently confirmed].

The hook is not "remittances vs. no remittances" — it's that the record
remittance total and the skilled-emigration cost estimate are, on the
evidence gathered here, **largely describing two different populations**:
the remittance record is built overwhelmingly on Gulf labour migration
(construction, transport, domestic service), while the "brain drain" cost
estimate is about a much smaller, higher-education cohort (doctors,
engineers, accountants, IT professionals) that BEOE's own registration
categories put at under 30,000 people a year — a rounding error next to
the roughly 700,000+ Pakistanis who register for overseas work annually
[SOURCE: BEOE data via multiple secondary reports, 2024-2025] [VERIFY].

## Ground-truth mechanics

- The **Bureau of Emigration & Overseas Employment (BEOE)** runs Pakistan's
  protectorate/registration system for anyone leaving on a work permit. It
  classifies emigrants into tiers: highly qualified, highly skilled,
  skilled, semi-skilled, unskilled [SOURCE: Gulf Research Center/KAS policy
  brief, 2025, directly read].
- **This registration system undercounts true skilled outflow.** BEOE data
  only captures people who leave on a work permit and register with the
  protectorate system. Anyone who leaves on a student visa, an immigrant
  visa, moves to the Gulf from a third country, or leaves on a visit visa
  and finds work after arrival is **not required to register** — meaning
  the "highly qualified"/"highly skilled" totals BEOE publishes are a
  floor, not a ceiling, on actual skilled emigration [SOURCE: Gulf
  Research Center/KAS policy brief, 2025, directly read].
- In 2023, about 862,000 people registered for overseas work in total, of
  whom ~96% went to the Gulf; Saudi Arabia took 49.5%, the UAE 26.7%, Qatar
  6.4% [SOURCE: Gulf Research Center/KAS policy brief, 2025, directly
  read]. More than half of all registered emigrants that year were
  classified unskilled or semi-skilled [SOURCE: same].
- BEOE's "highly skilled/qualified" share of total registered emigrants has
  stayed **below 10% for the last five decades** [SOURCE: Gulf Research
  Center/KAS policy brief, 2025, directly read].
- 2024 skill-level breakdown of registered emigrants: unskilled 366,092;
  skilled 255,706; semi-skilled 56,562; highly skilled 29,434; highly
  qualified 19,587 [SOURCE: PPA migration-data summary, 2026, citing
  BEOE/SBP figures] [VERIFY: secondary aggregator, not BEOE itself].

## Occupation-specific emigration figures (the "brain drain" cohort)

- **2025:** 3,795 doctors, 5,946 engineers, 5,659 accountants, 1,640
  nurses, 1,725 teachers emigrated [SOURCE: BEOE data via TechJuice/Minute
  Mirror, 2025-2026] [VERIFY: BEOE's own site returned 403 on direct
  fetch; relying on secondary reporting].
- **2024:** 3,642 doctors, 8,018 engineers emigrated [SOURCE: same lineage
  as above] [VERIFY].
- **Combined 2024-2025:** widely reported across outlets as "nearly 5,000
  doctors, 11,000 engineers, and more than 13,000 accountants" [SOURCE:
  Tribune India/ThePrint/NewsKarnataka, Dec 2025] [VERIFY: all ultimately
  citing the same BEOE release; the primary release itself was not
  directly accessible].
- **Nurses:** a Gulf Research Center/KAS policy brief (directly read, using
  BEOE data) states nurse emigration rose from **315 in 2013 to 4,880 in
  2023** [SOURCE: Gulf Research Center/KAS policy brief, 2025, directly
  read]. A separate WebSearch summary claimed nurse emigration rose
  "2,144% between 2011 and 2024" — this does not arithmetically match the
  315-to-4,880 figures (which would be roughly a 1,450% increase over a
  different ten-year window). **[VERIFY / UNRESOLVED]** — use the
  directly-read 315→4,880 (2013→2023) figures as the more defensible
  citation; do not use the "2,144%" figure until its source and base years
  are identified.
- Pakistan's own nurse-patient ratio in general hospital wards is
  reportedly about 1:40, against a Pakistan Nursing Council-recommended
  3:10 [SOURCE: Gulf Research Center/KAS policy brief, 2025, directly
  read, citing Hassan 2023].

## The internal inconsistency this episode should surface

The PIDE-linked "$250 million a year" training-investment-loss figure is
explicitly built on an assumption of **10,000 doctors emigrating annually**
[SOURCE: PIDE-lineage figure via Profit/Tribune/Daily Times, Jul 2025]
[VERIFY]. BEOE's own registered numbers for the same period show roughly
**3,600-3,800 doctors a year** — well under half that assumption
[SOURCE: BEOE data via TechJuice/Minute Mirror] [VERIFY]. Given BEOE's own
acknowledged undercount (student/immigrant-visa emigrants aren't
registered), the true number could plausibly sit somewhere between these
two figures, but there is no public reconciliation of exactly where. This
gap is worth stating honestly on screen rather than picking whichever
number is more dramatic — it's also a legitimately interesting beat: the
"authoritative" cost estimate that gets repeated in every article is built
on an assumption nearly 3x larger than the government's own registration
data for the same cohort.

## Cost side: what training a doctor is said to cost the state

- A PIDE-linked estimate puts the cost of training one doctor in Pakistan
  at roughly **$25,000** [SOURCE: PIDE-lineage figure via
  Profit/Tribune/Daily Times/Stratheia, Jul 2025] [VERIFY: methodology not
  shown in any source I could access; likely includes infrastructure and
  faculty cost, not just the tuition fee charged to the student].
- By contrast, the **fee a student actually pays** at a public medical
  college is nominal — roughly Rs 20,000-100,000 per year (about
  Rs 100,000-400,000 for the full 5-year MBBS), a small fraction of what
  a private-college seat costs (~Rs 8-9 million for 5 years) [SOURCE:
  multiple MBBS fee-structure aggregator sites, 2026] [VERIFY: these are
  student-facing fees, not government expenditure per seat — I could not
  locate a government budget document (HEC or provincial health
  department) giving the state's actual per-seat cost, so the $25,000
  figure cannot be independently reconstructed from public fee data
  alone]. Pakistan has roughly 4,800 public-sector MBBS seats nationally
  (Punjab ~2,400, Sindh ~1,200, KPK ~700, Balochistan ~500) [SOURCE: Maqsad
  Blog aggregator, 2026] [VERIFY].
- A separate, much larger PIDE estimate (Khan and Ahmad, 2024, as
  summarized in a PIDE-published piece by Henna Ahsan, Oct 2024, directly
  read) calculated Pakistan's total productivity loss from human capital
  migration at **US$303.4 billion in 2023 alone**, based on migrants'
  contribution to global GDP minus remittances received [SOURCE: PIDE
  (Ahsan, 2024), directly read]. **This figure needs careful framing if
  used at all** — at roughly 80% of Pakistan's entire GDP, it almost
  certainly reflects the cumulative productive value of the existing
  diaspora stock in one year, not the marginal cost of new emigration in
  2023 alone. I was not able to access the underlying Khan and Ahmad
  (2024) paper directly to confirm the methodology — [VERIFY] before using
  this number in the script; if used, it must be clearly distinguished
  from the $4.2-4.8 billion annual-flow figures, which describe something
  different.

## Workforce-capacity consequence

- Pakistan has **319,000 registered doctors** as of the Economic Survey
  2024-25 (released June 2025), plus 39,088 dentists, 138,000 nurses,
  46,801 midwives, and 29,000 lady health workers [SOURCE: Economic Survey
  2024-25 via Express Tribune, 2025] [VERIFY: secondary report, Economic
  Survey itself not directly opened].
- Against Pakistan's population of ~241.5 million, 319,000 doctors works
  out to roughly 1 doctor per 757 people nationally — broadly consistent
  with separately-reported figures of "about 1 doctor per 1,000
  population" against a WHO-recommended 3-4 per 1,000 [SOURCE: Friday
  Times/Agricultural Economist summaries, 2025] [VERIFY]. **One widely
  circulated WebSearch summary claimed a ratio of "1 doctor per 750,000
  people" — this is arithmetically inconsistent with the 319,000-doctor
  figure and should not be used; it is almost certainly a
  garbled/misreported statistic, possibly conflating a narrow
  specialist-per-facility count with the national physician ratio.**
- Rural areas fare far worse: reported rural doctor-to-population ratios
  as low as 1:3,500, and in some specific areas as extreme as 1:12,000
  (attributed to the Pakistan Medical Commission, 2023) [SOURCE: Friday
  Times/Agricultural Economist, 2025] [VERIFY: secondary reporting, PMC
  original source not directly accessed]. WHO recommends roughly 2.5
  medical staff (physicians, nurses, midwives combined) per 1,000 people
  for basic primary care [SOURCE: same].
- Only 25% of Pakistan's healthcare facilities are in rural areas (WHO,
  2023), and about 30% of Basic Health Units are non-functional, mainly
  from staffing shortages [SOURCE: Ministry of National Health Services
  data via secondary reporting, 2025] [VERIFY].
- As a historical illustration of cumulative stock loss (not current
  flow): Pakistan was, per 2016 OECD data, the second-largest source
  country of foreign-trained doctors in both the US (12,454 doctors, ~6%
  of all foreign doctors, after India's 45,830) and the UK (8,026 doctors,
  after India's 18,953) [SOURCE: OECD 2016 data, via riazhaq.com, directly
  read] [VERIFY: this is 2016 data, a decade old — cite only as historical
  context for the scale of accumulated medical-workforce emigration, not
  as a current figure].

## Broader macro impact

- Remittances are roughly **8% of Pakistan's GDP** [SOURCE: Gulf Research
  Center/KAS policy brief, 2025, directly read].
- FY2025-26 total remittances: **$41.6 billion**, up 8.6% from FY2024-25's
  **$38.3 billion** [SOURCE: Business Recorder, 2026] [VERIFY: secondary
  report, SBP's own remittance data page returned 403 on direct fetch].
  Top FY2025-26 sources: Saudi Arabia $9.78 billion, UAE $8.81 billion,
  UK $6.33 billion, EU countries $5.23 billion [SOURCE: same].
- In 2023, the three Gulf countries that receive 82% of all Pakistani
  emigrants (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar) contributed about **46% of all
  remittances** sent home that year [SOURCE: Gulf Research Center/KAS
  policy brief, 2025, directly read]. This is the direct evidentiary basis
  for the thesis's claim that Pakistan's remittance lifeline runs mainly
  through Gulf labour migration, not the "brain drain" cohort.
- Cumulative Gulf labour migration since 1971 is reported at over 15.1
  million registered workers, with Saudi Arabia alone accounting for
  roughly 7.87 million (52%) [SOURCE: PPA migration-data summary, 2026]
  [VERIFY: secondary aggregator].
- Graduate unemployment in Pakistan is reported at 16%, against an overall
  unemployment rate of about 5% [SOURCE: Ahsan and Khan, 2024, PIDE, as
  cited in Ahsan 2024, directly read]. A separate PIDE survey found 37% of
  respondents wanted to leave the country, rising to 78% in a follow-up
  survey when the question was tied to "for better income" [SOURCE: PIDE
  (Nayab 2022; Haque and Nayab 2021), as cited in Ahsan 2024, directly
  read]. These push-factor numbers matter for the counterarguments section
  below.

## Counterarguments

1. **Per-capita remittances from settled professional migrants may be
   higher than the Gulf average.** A WebSearch-summarized Express Tribune
   analysis states that while Gulf countries send larger *total*
   remittances due to sheer worker volume, individual UK-based
   professional migrants remit considerably more *per person* than the
   Gulf average [SOURCE: Express Tribune, "Remittances expose Gulf
   dependency," undated 2025-2026] [VERIFY: could not directly open this
   article — 403 on fetch; relying on WebSearch's summary only. This is
   the single most important claim to verify before scripting, since it
   directly complicates the thesis: if true, the skilled cohort's smaller
   aggregate remittance contribution may be explained mainly by its much
   smaller population size, not by lower individual generosity or
   attachment — a materially different, more honest framing than "they
   don't send money back."]
2. **The training-investment "loss" assumes a counterfactual that isn't
   established.** With graduate unemployment at 16% (more than 3x the
   overall rate), some share of emigrating professionals may have been
   underemployed or unemployed in Pakistan had they stayed — meaning the
   state's training investment may not have been converting into full
   domestic productive value even absent emigration. This doesn't erase
   the training-cost loss, but it complicates the "at home, this person
   would have been fully productive" assumption baked into the bigger
   cost estimates.
3. **Remittances, return migration, diaspora investment, and knowledge
   transfer aren't captured in a simple training-cost-vs-remittance
   framing.** None of the sources gathered here quantify these channels
   specifically for the skilled-emigrant cohort; this is a real gap, not
   just a rhetorical point the other side makes.
